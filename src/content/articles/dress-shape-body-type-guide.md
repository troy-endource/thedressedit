Body shape guides have been around forever, and most of them are not particularly useful. They reduce people to fruit metaphors, imply that certain proportions need correcting, and offer advice so generalised it applies to almost no one specifically. We're not doing that here.

This guide works from a simpler starting point: different dress shapes create different visual effects, and understanding those effects lets you make more informed choices. Whether you want to emphasise your waist, create balance across your silhouette, elongate your frame, or simply feel comfortable — that's your decision to make. This is information, not instruction.

## Before We Start

Every body shape guide risks implying some bodies need work. They don't. The purpose of understanding dress shapes isn't to disguise anything — it's to make getting dressed faster and more enjoyable. If you love how you look in a particular dress, that's the only guide that matters.

That said, most of us have tried on a dress that looked wonderful on the hanger but somehow didn't translate. That's not a body problem — it's a proportional mismatch between the cut and your measurements. Understanding why it happened helps you avoid it next time.

## The Reliable Silhouettes

Before body shapes: three cuts that tend to work well across many different proportions.

**The wrap dress** creates a V-neckline that elongates, a tie waist that sits at the narrowest point of the torso, and an A-line skirt that skims rather than clings below. It works across a wide range of body types because the structure naturally adjusts to the wearer's proportions rather than demanding the wearer adjust to it.

**The A-line midi** — fitted through the bodice and gradually widening from the waist or hips — creates a balanced silhouette without requiring a specific set of measurements. It's the most reliably well-received dress shape across all body types, and it's the cut the high street does best.

**The belted shirt dress** combines structure with ease in a way that works broadly. The collar frames the face, a defined waist creates shape, and the relaxed skirt is comfortable for a full day. It's one of the only dress shapes that reads appropriately for work, weekends, and weddings with minimal styling changes.

## Hourglass Shape

**The proportions:** Bust and hips roughly similar in measurement, with a noticeably narrower waist. Most fitted dresses are cut with this shape in mind, so the majority of classic silhouettes will feel natural.

**What works well:** Anything that defines the waist. Wrap dresses, belted midis, pencil dresses, fit-and-flare silhouettes. V-necklines and scoop necks work particularly well by echoing the natural lines of the frame. Fabrics with some drape and movement follow the body beautifully — silk, viscose, and jersey all suit this shape.

**Worth knowing:** Very boxy or oversized shapes can obscure the waist that creates the silhouette. If you want a relaxed fit, add a loose belt to retain some structure. Very stiff fabrics that hold away from the body can feel at odds with curves — look for something with a little give.

**Brands to explore:** Reiss (excellent fitted midis), Karen Millen (strong pencil and wrap options), Whistles (good wrap dress range), and Mango's satin collection.

## Pear Shape

**The proportions:** Hips wider than shoulders, with weight carried predominantly below the waist. One of the most common proportions in the UK, and one the fashion industry has historically underserved — though that's been changing.

**What works well:** A-line and fit-and-flare cuts skim over the hips rather than clinging to them. Dresses with interesting necklines, sleeve details, or upper-body detail draw the eye upward and create visual balance. Square necklines, puff sleeves, and statement collars all do this effectively. Darker tones below the waist with lighter or more detailed fabric above can produce a well-balanced effect.

**Worth knowing:** Bodycon or very fitted skirts can feel restrictive and highlight proportional differences in a way that often feels uncomfortable. This isn't about hiding hips — it's about choosing cuts that skim rather than grip, which is both more comfortable and more considered. Tulip skirts and dropped waists can create an unbalanced line.

**Brands to explore:** & Other Stories (beautiful fit-and-flare cuts), COS (well-structured A-lines), Nobody's Child (good proportion work at an accessible price), and L.K.Bennett for special occasions.

## Apple Shape

**The proportions:** Weight carried predominantly around the middle, with slimmer legs and arms. The aim is usually to create a sense of flow through the torso rather than defining a waist that doesn't read as narrow.

**What works well:** Empire-line dresses — fitted under the bust, then falling freely — work exceptionally well because they define the narrowest point above the waist and let the fabric drape from there. V-necklines elongate the torso. Column dresses in fluid fabric that skim the body create a clean, unbroken line. Shirt dresses worn slightly open at the neck with a loose belt sitting at the hip (rather than the natural waist) are a particularly strong option.

**Worth knowing:** Wide belts at the natural waist can create a visual pinch that draws attention rather than deflecting it. Very structured or stiff fabric that holds away from the body in the midsection can add visual weight. Peplums are worth trying — some apple-shaped people love the definition they offer; others find them unflattering. Only your mirror can tell you.

**Brands to explore:** ARKET (relaxed, draped cuts with good structure), Mint Velvet (excellent fluid dresses), Whistles (strong shirt dress range), and Free People for more relaxed occasions.

## Rectangle Shape

**The proportions:** Shoulders, waist, and hips roughly similar in width, creating a straight silhouette without a dramatically defined waist. This is one of the most versatile shapes for modern dressing — most minimalist and contemporary dress designs are cut with this frame in mind.

**What works well:** Two directions are available. To introduce waist definition: wrap dresses, belted styles, or peplum waists create curves. To lean into the straight silhouette: column dresses, shift dresses, and relaxed shirt dresses look clean and intentional on a rectangular frame — this is the body shape COS and ARKET largely design for. Layering (a dress with a structured jacket, or an unbuttoned overshirt) adds visual dimension that works particularly well here.

**Worth knowing:** Very fitted dresses designed to follow curves may not have enough to follow, and can look slightly flat. Create interest through structure instead — architectural details, pleating, and defined shoulders add visual presence without relying on body-hugging fabric.

**Brands to explore:** COS (built for this frame), ARKET, & Other Stories, and Jigsaw's more structured pieces.

## Inverted Triangle Shape

**The proportions:** Shoulders wider than hips, often with a fuller bust. The visual weight sits in the upper body; if you want to create balance, the approach is to add interest or volume below the waist.

**What works well:** Full skirts, A-lines, and anything with volume from the waist down create a balanced effect. V-necklines and scoop necks work better than boat necks or high necklines, which can read wide across broad shoulders. Darker tones on top with lighter or printed fabric below naturally shift visual focus downward.

**Worth knowing:** Puff sleeves, very wide necklines, and heavily embellished bodices add visual width to an already broader upper body. Halter necks and strapless dresses can emphasise shoulder width, though strapless can also look spectacular with the right support — it depends on your preference and the occasion.

**Brands to explore:** Reiss (strong V-neck options), Phase Eight (excellent for occasion dresses that create proportional balance), Hobbs, and Boden.

## Petite

**The proportions:** Under 5'3" (160cm), regardless of body shape. The challenge isn't your body — it's that most high street dresses are cut for someone 5'5"–5'7", which means hemlines, waist placements, and proportions often sit in the wrong position.

**What works well:** Petite ranges are worth seeking out because the proportions are adjusted throughout the garment, not just the length. Higher waistlines, V-necklines, and vertical details (front buttons, centre seams) create the appearance of length. Midi dresses that hit at the shin work well; too long and they can visually shorten a petite frame.

**Worth knowing:** Very large prints and very wide silhouettes can overwhelm smaller frames. Smaller-scale prints and fitted or gently A-line cuts tend to sit more naturally. Proportional layering — a shorter jacket rather than a long one — also makes a significant difference.

**Brands with strong petite ranges:** Hobbs, John Lewis, Boden, and Phase Eight all offer well-considered petite edits with proper proportional adjustment.

## Finding Your Brands

The most practically useful advice in this guide: once you find a brand that consistently works for your proportions, it's worth returning to it rather than searching from scratch each time. Brands cut differently — COS suits straighter frames, Reiss is strong for defined waists, Phase Eight and Hobbs offer the widest range of occasion fits. Finding the brands that fit your body and shopping there first saves more time than any shape guide.

## The Bottom Line

The best dress for your body is the one you stop thinking about twenty minutes after you put it on — because you're comfortable, confident, and can get on with everything else. Body shape guides are useful shortcuts for navigating a fitting room, not rules for what you're allowed to wear. Trust the mirror over the theory, and if you love it, wear it.
