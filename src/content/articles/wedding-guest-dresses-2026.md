Wedding season doesn't so much arrive as descend. One save-the-date becomes three, then five, and suddenly your weekends from May to September are spoken for. The question of what to wear — which once felt like the fun part — starts to feel like a logistical puzzle. You need something that works for a barn in the Cotswolds and a rooftop in Shoreditch, that photographs well and travels better, that makes you feel genuinely good without drawing the wrong kind of attention.

We've reviewed the full landscape of wedding guest dressing for 2026 — every major retailer, every relevant trend, every practical consideration — and pulled it into one guide. ## The new rules

Before the dresses, a quick update on what's shifted — and what hasn't. The old conventions still apply: avoid white, read the dress code carefully, dress for the venue. But 2026 has introduced a few things worth knowing.

**The midi has settled in as the default length.** It works across every type of wedding, every dress code, every season. Flattering across body types, practical on grass, polished in photographs. If you're buying one wedding guest dress this year, make it a midi.

**Jewel tones are the colour story for autumn and winter.** Burgundy, forest green, sapphire, chocolate brown. Richer than last year's pastels and considerably more photogenic in low light. If pastels wash you out, this is good news.

**Texture has replaced embellishment as the way to add interest.** Pleated silk, jacquard weave, crinkled crepe, subtle sheer. These details reward close attention without announcing themselves across a room — which is exactly right.

## Summer weddings

Summer weddings call for colour, movement and fabric that actually breathes. Cotton and linen earn their place here, though a well-cut viscose or Tencel blend travels better without the creasing. The dress should feel easy — not like something you're managing.

For May and June, a floral midi in cotton or linen is a reliable starting point. Keep the print considered rather than bold — watercolour botanicals, tonal florals, soft ditsy prints all work well. Pair with a block heel or wedge you can genuinely walk on grass in.

For July and August, when temperatures climb, fabric becomes the priority. A simple slip dress in a lightweight satin, or a relaxed shirt dress in cotton, will carry you through a long afternoon without complaint. Avoid heavy lining or anything too structured.

**Colours for summer:** Sage, soft olive, blush, warm coral, sky blue, lavender. Butter yellow has had a strong season — it photographs beautifully and works across a wide range of skin tones. A solid colour is easier to restyle across multiple weddings than a distinctive print.

## Formal and black tie weddings

When the invitation says black tie, resist the impulse to buy something entirely new. The more considered approach is a dress that earns its place across several occasions — a column midi in navy or emerald, for instance, reads differently with every jewellery combination.

Look for fabrics with drape rather than structure: silk, satin, and crepe all move well and photograph beautifully in evening light. A bias-cut slip in a deep jewel tone is endlessly adaptable — add a velvet wrap for winter formality, or let it stand alone in summer.

**On embellishment:** a single considered detail — chandelier earrings, a beaded clutch, a sculptural heel — often does more work than all-over sequins. The aim is polished, not competing.

Before investing over £200, ask the three-event test: can this dress work for a winter wedding, a work dinner, and a birthday celebration? A navy satin midi passes easily. Most novelty pieces don't.

## Country and barn weddings

The country wedding is the hardest dress code to read correctly. Too formal and you'll look at odds with the setting; too casual and you'll feel underdressed beside the bridal party. The balance is a dress with structure but softness — a cotton midi with interesting sleeves, a printed tea dress in a warm tone, a linen wrap style that moves well.

Florals work here, but aim for something quieter — watercolour prints, ditsy patterns, botanical drawings. Earthy tones (olive, terracotta, dusty rose, mustard) tend to suit rural settings and look good in golden-hour photographs.

Practicality matters more at country weddings than anywhere else. You may be walking across fields, sitting on hay bales, dancing on uneven ground. Wedge espadrilles, block-heel sandals, or a well-chosen flat are better options than a heel that will sink into the lawn. Check whether you can sit, walk, and dance in the dress before committing to it.

## City weddings

A city wedding — London hotel, Edinburgh townhouse, Manchester loft — rewards a bit more edge. Sharper silhouettes, bolder colours, more modern fabrications. A block-colour midi in cobalt or deep rust, an architectural one-shoulder dress, a clean tailored jumpsuit. The urban backdrop suits confidence.

Practicality is less of a concern: heels work on polished floors, structured fabrics hold their shape in air-conditioned rooms, and a clutch is perfectly adequate when you're not navigating outdoor terrain. This is where you can experiment more freely.

For an evening city wedding, don't be afraid of a strong accessory. Statement earrings with a clean neckline, or a sculptural bag against a simple dress — one considered focal point carries an outfit further than trying to do everything at once.

## Autumn and winter weddings

Late-season weddings call for richer, more considered dressing. Velvet, heavy crepe, and textured knit replace summer's linens, while colours deepen to jewel tones and warm neutrals. Burgundy, forest green, midnight blue, chocolate brown, deep teal. A well-chosen metallic — bronze, antique gold — adds warmth without overwhelming.

Layering is part of the outfit at this time of year. A well-cut coat or a cashmere wrap matters as much as the dress itself, particularly for any outdoor photography. Think about whether they work together before leaving the house.

**On fabric for winter:** Velvet drapes beautifully, photographs richly, and provides just enough warmth for a draughty church. Heavy crepe holds colour intensely and suits jewel tones particularly well. If you prefer satin or silk, choose a heavier weight — charmeuse rather than habotai — so the dress reads as deliberate rather than summery.

## Under £100

The UK high street consistently delivers well-priced wedding guest dresses that look considered and travel without drama. A few brands worth knowing:

**COS** (£65–£115): Structured shapes in excellent fabrics. Their pleated dresses are reliable — formal enough for a ceremony, comfortable enough for dancing.

**& Other Stories** (£55–£120): More print-forward, with considered florals and interesting sleeve details. Their wrap dresses fit well and hold their shape.

**Mango** (£30–£80): Strong occasion range with good satin and crepe pieces at genuinely reasonable prices.

**ARKET** (£55–£95): Understated and well made. Particularly good for simple shapes in quality jersey and linen blends.

**Hush** (£60–£120): Relaxed but polished. Their column maxis and jersey midis have a quiet confidence that works well for country and garden weddings.

The principle across all of them: look for a simple, well-cut shape in a good fabric. A plain satin midi will always read as more considered than a heavily embellished polyester dress at the same price point.

## What to avoid

A few things remain constant regardless of the year:

White, ivory, cream, or anything that reads as bridal — this includes pale champagne and blush that could be confused for it. Anything too revealing for a ceremony setting. An all-black dress at a sunny daytime garden wedding (black is entirely appropriate for evening and winter, but can read as funereal against August sunshine). Any dress you haven't sat down in before the day.

If you know the bridal party colours, choose something that doesn't clash or match exactly. And if the invitation says casual, it still means considered — it doesn't mean denim.
